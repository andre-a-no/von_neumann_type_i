# 4. Symmetries and representations

Chapter 2 showed that a symmetry group produces a von Neumann algebra, the span of the symmetry
operators, together with its commutant, the algebra of symmetric observables (Proposition 2.7), and
decomposed one example by hand. This chapter supplies the representation theory behind that
example, turns it into an algorithm that the library runs on any list of matrices, and applies it
to the two symmetries of spin systems: $U(1)$, whose charge is a particle number or a magnetisation,
and $SU(2)$, the rotations of spins.

**You will learn** complete reducibility and Schur's lemma, how the decomposition into irreducibles
determines the commutant and the generated algebra, how characters count multiplicities, why a random
element of the commutant finds the decomposition numerically, what charge sectors are, the irreducible
spin representations and how $L$ spins $\frac12$ decompose, and why $SU(2)$-invariant Hamiltonians have
much smaller blocks than $U(1)$-invariant ones.

Throughout, $S'$ is the commutant of a set $S\subseteq M_n(\mathbb C)$ (Chapter 2). If $S^*=S$, then
$S''$ is the **von Neumann algebra generated by** $S$: it is a $*$-algebra containing $S$, and every
$*$-algebra $\mathcal B\supseteq S$ satisfies $\mathcal B=\mathcal B''\supseteq S''$ (Theorem 2.2).
$\mathcal A'_h=\{K\in\mathcal A':K=K^*\}$, a real vector space. $\mathbb C^k\otimes\mathbb C^m$ carries
the Kronecker order of Chapter 7: $e_a\otimes f_j$ is basis vector number $am+j$ ($0$-based), so
$1_k\otimes x$ is block diagonal with $k$ copies of $x$. $|G|$ is the order of a finite group, and
$\hbar=1$.

## 4.1 Representations and Schur's lemma

**Definition.** Let $X$ be a set with an involution $x\mapsto x^*$ ($x^{**}=x$). A
**representation** of $X$ on $\mathbb C^k$ is a map $\rho:X\to M_k(\mathbb C)$ with
$\rho(x^*)=\rho(x)^*$. A subspace $K$ is **invariant** if $\rho(x)K\subseteq K$ for all $x$; $\rho$ is
**irreducible** if $k\ge1$ and $0,\mathbb C^k$ are the only invariant subspaces. An
**intertwiner** from $\rho$ to a representation $\rho'$ on $\mathbb C^{k'}$ is a $k'\times k$ matrix
$T$ with $T\rho(x)=\rho'(x)T$ for all $x$; they form a vector space $\operatorname{Hom}(\rho,\rho')$,
and $\operatorname{Hom}(\rho,\rho)=\rho(X)'$. $\rho$ and $\rho'$ are **equivalent**, $\rho\cong\rho'$, if
there is an invertible intertwiner.

Three cases occur: a group $G$ with $g^*=g^{-1}$, where we require $\rho$ to be a homomorphism
$G\to U(k)$ (a **unitary representation**; then $\rho(g^{-1})=\rho(g)^{-1}=\rho(g)^*$); a $*$-algebra with
its adjoint, where $\rho$ is a unital $*$-homomorphism; and labels of generators with $x^*=x$, e.g.
$\{x,y,z\}$, represented by tuples of Hermitian matrices such as spin matrices (Section 4.5). If $K$ is invariant
and the columns of $Q\in M_{k\times d}$ are an orthonormal basis of $K$, then $\rho(x)Q=QQ^*\rho(x)Q$, so
$\rho_K(x)=Q^*\rho(x)Q$ is again a representation (a homomorphism if $\rho$ is one), the **restriction**
of $\rho$ to $K$.

**Lemma 4.1 (complete reducibility).** Let $\rho$ be a representation of $X$ on $\mathbb C^n$.
(a) If $K$ is invariant, so is $K^\perp$. (b) $\mathbb C^n$ is an orthogonal direct sum of invariant
subspaces on each of which $\rho$ is irreducible.

*Proof.* (a) For $y\in K^\perp$ and $z\in K$, $\langle z,\rho(x)y\rangle=\langle\rho(x^*)z,y\rangle=0$, since
$\rho(x^*)z\in K$. (b) Induction on $n$. A nonzero invariant subspace $K_1$ of minimal dimension is
irreducible; $K_1^\perp$ is invariant by (a), and the invariant subspaces of the restriction to
$K_1^\perp$ are invariant subspaces of $\rho$, so the induction hypothesis applies to it. $\square$

For a finite group, unitarity costs nothing: if the $\rho(g)$ are merely invertible, then
$P=\sum_g\rho(g)^*\rho(g)>0$ satisfies $\rho(h)^*P\rho(h)=\sum_g\rho(gh)^*\rho(gh)=P$, so
$P^{1/2}\rho(h)P^{-1/2}$ is unitary (Weyl's trick; for compact groups the sum becomes a Haar integral).

**Lemma 4.2 (Schur's lemma).** Let $\rho,\rho'$ be irreducible representations of $X$ on
$\mathbb C^k,\mathbb C^{k'}$ and $T\in\operatorname{Hom}(\rho,\rho')$.
(a) $T=0$ or $T$ is invertible. (b) $\operatorname{Hom}(\rho,\rho)=\mathbb C1$.
(c) $T^*\in\operatorname{Hom}(\rho',\rho)$, and if $T\neq0$, then $T=\lambda U$ with $\lambda>0$ and $U$ unitary.
Hence $\dim\operatorname{Hom}(\rho,\rho')$ is $1$ if $\rho\cong\rho'$ and $0$ otherwise, and equivalent
irreducible representations are unitarily equivalent.

*Proof.* (a) and (b) were proved for groups after Proposition 2.7, and the proof only uses that
$\ker T$ is invariant under $\rho$ ($T\rho(x)v=\rho'(x)Tv=0$ if $Tv=0$) and the range of $T$ under
$\rho'$: so $\ker T\in\{0,\mathbb C^k\}$ and $\operatorname{ran}T\in\{0,\mathbb C^{k'}\}$; and for (b), $T-\lambda1$
with $\lambda$ an eigenvalue of $T$ is a non-invertible intertwiner, hence $0$. (c) The adjoint of
$T\rho(x^*)=\rho'(x^*)T$ is $\rho(x)T^*=T^*\rho'(x)$. Then $T^*T\in\operatorname{Hom}(\rho,\rho)$, so
$T^*T=\lambda^21$ with $\lambda>0$ if $T\neq0$ ($T^*T\ge0$ is nonzero), and $U=T/\lambda$ is square by
(a) with $U^*U=1$. Finally, if $T_1,T_2\in\operatorname{Hom}(\rho,\rho')$ are nonzero, then
$T_1^{-1}T_2\in\operatorname{Hom}(\rho,\rho)=\mathbb C1$. $\square$

If all $\rho(x)$ commute with each other (an abelian group), an irreducible $\rho$ is one-dimensional:
every $\rho(x)$ lies in $\operatorname{Hom}(\rho,\rho)=\mathbb C1$, so every subspace is invariant.

## 4.2 The isotypic decomposition and the commutant

**Theorem 4.3 (isotypic decomposition).** Let $\rho$ be a representation of $X$ on $\mathbb C^n$.
There are irreducible, pairwise inequivalent representations $\rho_1,\dots,\rho_C$ of $X$ on
$\mathbb C^{k_c}$, integers $m_c\ge1$ with $\sum_ck_cm_c=n$, and a unitary
$V:\bigoplus_c\mathbb C^{k_c}\otimes\mathbb C^{m_c}\to\mathbb C^n$ with
$V^*\rho(x)V=\bigoplus_c\rho_c(x)\otimes1_{m_c}$ for all $x$. For every such decomposition
$$
V^*\rho(X)'V=\bigoplus_{c=1}^C1_{k_c}\otimes M_{m_c}(\mathbb C),\qquad
V^*\rho(X)''V=\bigoplus_{c=1}^CM_{k_c}(\mathbb C)\otimes1_{m_c},
$$
and $m_c=\dim\operatorname{Hom}(\rho_c,\rho)$; an irreducible representation $\sigma$ of $X$ has
$\operatorname{Hom}(\sigma,\rho)\neq0$ iff $\sigma\cong\rho_c$ for exactly one $c$.

*Proof.* *Existence.* Lemma 4.1 gives pairwise orthogonal invariant subspaces $W_1,\dots,W_M$ with sum
$\mathbb C^n$ and irreducible restrictions $\sigma_i(x)=Q_i^*\rho(x)Q_i$. Group the $W_i$ into classes of
equivalent restrictions and choose in each class $c$ a reference $\rho_c=\sigma_{i_0}$. For each other
$i$ in the class, Lemma 4.2(c) gives a unitary $U_i$ with $\sigma_i(x)=U_i\rho_c(x)U_i^*$; replacing $Q_i$
by $Q_iU_i$ makes $Q_i^*\rho(x)Q_i=\rho_c(x)$. Number the members of class $c$ by $j=0,\dots,m_c-1$, write
$Q_{c,j}$ for their bases, and let $V(e_a\otimes f_j)=Q_{c,j}e_a$ on the $c$-th summand. As the $W_i$ are
invariant and orthogonal, $Q_{c,j}^*\rho(x)Q_{d,l}=\delta_{cd}\delta_{jl}\rho_c(x)$, which is the claimed form.

*Commutant.* $\rho(x)Q_{d,l}=Q_{d,l}\rho_d(x)$ and $Q_{c,j}^*\rho(x)=\rho_c(x)Q_{c,j}^*$ (invariance, and its
adjoint applied to $x^*$). Since $V$ is unitary, $Y\in M_n$ commutes with all $\rho(x)$ iff every block
$Y_{cj,dl}=Q_{c,j}^*YQ_{d,l}$ satisfies $Y_{cj,dl}\rho_d(x)=\rho_c(x)Y_{cj,dl}$, i.e. lies in
$\operatorname{Hom}(\rho_d,\rho_c)$, which is $0$ for $c\neq d$ and $\mathbb C1$ for $c=d$ (Lemma 4.2). So
$Y\in\rho(X)'$ iff $Y_{cj,dl}=\delta_{cd}(y_c)_{jl}1_{k_c}$, i.e. $V^*YV=\bigoplus_c1_{k_c}\otimes y_c$.

*Double commutant.* If $Z$ commutes with $\rho(X)'$, it commutes with the projections onto the
summands (take $y_c=1$ and $y_d=0$ for $d\neq c$), so $V^*ZV=\bigoplus_cZ_c$, and $Z_c$ commutes with
$1_{k_c}\otimes M_{m_c}$; by Lemma 2.5, Step 5 (with the two tensor factors exchanged),
$Z_c\in M_{k_c}\otimes1_{m_c}$. Conversely these operators commute with $\rho(X)'$.

*Multiplicities.* For $T\in\operatorname{Hom}(\sigma,\rho)$ with $\sigma$ irreducible, the same argument shows
$Q_{c,j}^*T\in\operatorname{Hom}(\sigma,\rho_c)$, which is $\mathbb Ct_{c}$ with $t_c$ invertible if $\sigma\cong\rho_c$ and
$0$ otherwise; and $T=\sum_{c,j}Q_{c,j}Q_{c,j}^*T$. As the $\rho_c$ are pairwise inequivalent, $\sigma$ is
equivalent to at most one of them, say $\rho_c$ (otherwise $T=0$), and then $T=\sum_ja_jQ_{c,j}t_c$; conversely
each $Q_{c,j}t_c$ lies in $\operatorname{Hom}(\sigma,\rho)$, and these $m_c$ maps have orthogonal ranges, so
$\dim\operatorname{Hom}(\sigma,\rho)=m_c$. $\square$

For a group, $\rho(G)''$ is the span of $\rho(G)$ (Section 2.4), and Theorem 4.3 is Proposition 2.7 read in
the other direction: there the structure theorem produced the irreducible representations, here they
produce the structure; in both, $\dim\rho(X)'=\sum_cm_c^2$ and $\dim\rho(X)''=\sum_ck_c^2$. The range of
$P_c=\sum_jQ_{c,j}Q_{c,j}^*$, a minimal central projection of $\rho(X)''$, is the **isotypic component** of $\rho_c$.

> **Physical meaning.** If $\rho(X)$ is a symmetry, every symmetric Hamiltonian $H\in\rho(X)'$ is
> $V(\bigoplus_c1_{k_c}\otimes h_c)V^*$: the eigenvalues of the $m_c\times m_c$ block $h_c$ appear $k_c$
> times each. The $k_c$-fold degenerate levels are the **multiplets** of the symmetry, and the blocks one
> has to diagonalise have the sizes $m_c$ of the multiplicities, not the sizes of the multiplets.

For a finite group the multiplicities can be computed without decomposing anything.

**Theorem 4.4 (Schur orthogonality).** Let $\rho,\rho'$ be irreducible unitary representations of a
finite group $G$ on $\mathbb C^k,\mathbb C^{k'}$. Then
$$
\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\rho(g)_{ij}\,\overline{\rho'(g)_{i'j'}}=
\begin{cases}\delta_{ii'}\delta_{jj'}/k&\text{if }\rho=\rho',\\0&\text{if }\rho\not\cong\rho'.\end{cases}
$$

*Proof.* For a $k\times k'$ matrix $Y$ let $T=\frac1{|G|}\sum_g\rho(g)Y\rho'(g)^*$. For $h\in G$, substituting
$g\to hg$ and using $\rho'(hg)^*=\rho'(g)^*\rho'(h)^{-1}$ gives $\rho(h)T=T\rho'(h)$, so
$T\in\operatorname{Hom}(\rho',\rho)$. If $\rho\not\cong\rho'$, $T=0$; if $\rho=\rho'$, $T=\lambda1$ with
$k\lambda=\operatorname{Tr}T=\operatorname{Tr}Y$ (Lemma 4.2). For $Y=|j\rangle\langle j'|$, the entry $T_{ii'}$
is the left side, and $\operatorname{Tr}Y=\delta_{jj'}$. $\square$

The **character** of a representation is $\chi_\rho(g)=\operatorname{Tr}\rho(g)$; equivalent representations
have equal characters, since $\operatorname{Tr}(T\rho(g)T^{-1})=\operatorname{Tr}\rho(g)$. Write
$\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle_G=\frac1{|G|}\sum_g\overline{\varphi(g)}\psi(g)$.

**Corollary 4.5 (characters).** Let $G$ be finite.
(a) For irreducible unitary $\rho,\rho'$: $\langle\chi_\rho,\chi_{\rho'}\rangle_G$ is $1$ if $\rho\cong\rho'$ and $0$ otherwise.
(b) For a unitary representation $\pi$ decomposed as in Theorem 4.3 and an irreducible $\rho$, the
multiplicity of $\rho$ in $\pi$ is
$$
m_\rho=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}\overline{\chi_\rho(g)}\,\chi_\pi(g)
$$
($m_\rho=m_c$ if $\rho\cong\rho_c$, and $m_\rho=0$ if $\rho$ is equivalent to no $\rho_c$).
(c) $\dim\pi(G)'=\sum_cm_c^2=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}|\chi_\pi(g)|^2$. In particular $\pi$ is irreducible iff
this number is $1$.

*Proof.* (a) Replace $\rho'$ by an equivalent representation (same character). Sum Theorem 4.4 over
$i=j$, $i'=j'$ and conjugate: the result is $\sum_{i,i'}\delta_{ii'}/k=1$ if $\rho=\rho'$, and $0$ if
$\rho\not\cong\rho'$. (b) The trace is unitarily invariant and $\operatorname{Tr}(a\otimes1_m)=m\operatorname{Tr}a$, so
$\chi_\pi=\sum_cm_c\chi_{\rho_c}$; apply (a). (c) By the same expansion and (a),
$\langle\chi_\pi,\chi_\pi\rangle_G=\sum_cm_c^2$, which is $\dim\pi(G)'$ by Theorem 4.3. $\square$

A second proof of (c) is how the library samples commutants. The **twirl**
$P(Y)=\frac1{|G|}\sum_g\pi(g)Y\pi(g)^*$ is idempotent with range $\pi(G)'$ (Chapter 2, Exercise 3) and
self-adjoint for $\langle Y,Z\rangle_{HS}=\operatorname{Tr}(Y^*Z)$ (substitute $g\to g^{-1}$), hence the orthogonal
projection onto $\pi(G)'$, and $\dim\pi(G)'$ is its trace. The map $Y\mapsto AYB$ has trace
$\operatorname{Tr}A\operatorname{Tr}B$ (its diagonal entries are $A_{ii}B_{jj}$), which gives $\frac1{|G|}\sum_g|\chi_\pi(g)|^2$.

We redo the example of Section 2.4 with characters. A permutation matrix has as trace the number of
basis vectors it fixes, and $\pi(\sigma)$ fixes $|b_1b_2b_3\rangle$ iff $b$ is constant on the cycles of
$\sigma$, so $\chi_\pi(\sigma)=2^{\#\text{cycles}(\sigma)}$. The group $S_3$ has the trivial, the sign and the
standard representation (the permutation representation on $\mathbb C^3$ minus the trivial one), with
characters $1$, $\operatorname{sgn}\sigma=(-1)^{3-\#\text{cycles}}$ and $\#\text{fixed points}-1$; they are real.

```python
import itertools
import math
import torch
import torch_vn_algebra.decompose as dc

torch.manual_seed(0)
dt = torch.complex128

def close(X, Y, tol=1e-10):
    return (torch.as_tensor(X) - torch.as_tensor(Y)).abs().max().item() < tol

def n_cycles(p):                                         # number of cycles of a permutation
    seen, count = set(), 0
    for i in range(len(p)):
        count += i not in seen
        while i not in seen:
            seen.add(i)
            i = p[i]
    return count

perms = list(itertools.permutations(range(3)))          # S_3, in the order of the library
G = dc.permutation_representation(3, 2)                  # (6, 8, 8): pi(sigma) permutes three qubits
chi_pi = G.diagonal(dim1=-2, dim2=-1).sum(-1).real       # characters Tr pi(sigma)
assert close(chi_pi, torch.tensor([2.0 ** n_cycles(p) for p in perms], dtype=torch.float64))
irreps = {'trivial': [1.0] * 6,
          'sign': [(-1.0) ** (3 - n_cycles(p)) for p in perms],
          'standard': [sum(p[i] == i for i in range(3)) - 1.0 for p in perms]}
chis = torch.tensor(list(irreps.values()), dtype=torch.float64)
assert close(chis @ chis.T / 6, torch.eye(3, dtype=torch.float64))    # Corollary 4.5(a): all irreducible
mult = chis @ chi_pi / 6                                               # Corollary 4.5(b)
print(dict(zip(irreps, mult.tolist())), (chi_pi ** 2).mean().item())
assert close(mult, torch.tensor([4.0, 0.0, 2.0], dtype=torch.float64))
assert dc.commutant_dimension(G) == 20                                # Corollary 4.5(c): M_4 (+) M_2
```

The trivial representation occurs $4$ times, the sign representation not at all, the standard one
twice; since $4\cdot1+2\cdot2=8$, nothing else occurs, and $\dim\pi(S_3)'=\frac16(64+3\cdot16+2\cdot4)=20$.
This is the decomposition of Section 2.4, now obtained from a table of six numbers per representation.

## 4.3 From generators to the algebra: a randomised algorithm

Theorem 4.3 describes the decomposition but does not compute it: its proof needs the irreducible
invariant subspaces. Murota, Kanno, Kojima and Kojima (*Japan J. Indust. Appl. Math.* 27 (2010) 125–160;
see also Maehara and Murota, *ibid.* 263–293) observed that the eigenspaces of a *random element of the
commutant* find them. Let $\mathcal A$ be the von Neumann algebra generated by given matrices
$S_1,\dots,S_J$ and their adjoints, with a decomposition $V^*\mathcal AV=\bigoplus_cM_{k_c}\otimes1_{m_c}$,
$V^*\mathcal A'V=\bigoplus_c1_{k_c}\otimes M_{m_c}$ (Theorem 2.6); only the analysis uses $V$.

**Lemma 4.6.** Let $K\in\mathcal A'_h$ with $V^*KV=\bigoplus_c1_{k_c}\otimes\kappa_c$. Every eigenspace of
$K$ is $\mathcal A$-invariant. If the $N=\sum_cm_c$ eigenvalues of all the $\kappa_c$ together are pairwise
distinct, the eigenspaces of $K$ are the $N$ subspaces $V(\mathbb C^{k_c}\otimes e)$ (in the $c$-th summand,
$e$ an eigenvector of $\kappa_c$), and $\mathcal A$ acts on each of them irreducibly, as $M_{k_c}$.

*Proof.* If $Kv=\lambda v$ and $a\in\mathcal A$, then $Kav=aKv=\lambda av$. Write
$\kappa_c=\sum_j\mu_{cj}|e_{cj}\rangle\langle e_{cj}|$ with orthonormal $e_{cj}$ (Theorem 1.3). Then
$V^*KV=\sum_{c,j}\mu_{cj}R_{cj}$, where $R_{cj}$ is the projection onto $\mathbb C^{k_c}\otimes e_{cj}$; the
$R_{cj}$ are orthogonal with sum $1$ and the $\mu_{cj}$ distinct, so these are the spectral projections. On
$\mathbb C^{k_c}\otimes e_{cj}$ the element $V^*aV=\bigoplus_da_d\otimes1$ acts as $a_c$, and the $a_c$ fill
$M_{k_c}$, which has no invariant subspaces but $0$ and $\mathbb C^{k_c}$. $\square$

**Proposition 4.7 (genericity).** Let $K$ and $Y$ be independent random elements of $\mathcal A'_h$ and of
$\mathcal A'$ whose laws have densities with respect to Lebesgue measure on these real vector spaces. With
probability one: (a) the hypothesis of Lemma 4.6 holds; (b) $\Pi_iY\Pi_j\neq0$ for any two spectral
projections $\Pi_i,\Pi_j$ of $K$ whose ranges lie in the same summand.

*Proof.* We use: *a real polynomial $p\not\equiv0$ on $\mathbb R^d$ vanishes only on a Lebesgue null set.*
For $d=1$ it has finitely many roots. For $d>1$ write $p=\sum_jp_j(x')t^j$ with $x'\in\mathbb R^{d-1}$ and
some $p_j\not\equiv0$; outside the null set $\{p_j=0\}$ (induction) the section $\{t:p(x',t)=0\}$ is
finite, and Tonelli's theorem gives measure $0$.

(a) The block matrix $\kappa=\bigoplus_c\kappa_c\in M_N$ is Hermitian and its entries are real-linear
functions of $K$. Let $\delta(K)=\det[\operatorname{Tr}\kappa^{i+j}]_{i,j=0}^{N-1}$, the Gram determinant of
$1,\kappa,\dots,\kappa^{N-1}$ for $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{HS}$; it is a real polynomial in the
coordinates of $K$, and $\delta(K)\neq0$ iff these powers are linearly independent. With
$\kappa=U\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)U^*$, $p(\kappa)=U\operatorname{diag}(p(\lambda_l))U^*$, so a nonzero polynomial of
degree $<N$ annihilates $\kappa$ iff it vanishes on $\operatorname{spec}\kappa$, which is possible iff $\kappa$ has
fewer than $N$ distinct eigenvalues ($\prod_\mu(t-\mu)$ over the distinct ones). So $\delta(K)\neq0$ is the
hypothesis of Lemma 4.6. $\delta$ is not identically zero: it is nonzero at
$V(\bigoplus_c1\otimes\operatorname{diag}(\text{distinct reals}))V^*\in\mathcal A'_h$. Hence $\{\delta=0\}$ is a null
set and has probability $0$.

(b) On the open set where (a) holds, the spectral projections are polynomials in $K$ whose coefficients
are continuous functions of the eigenvalues (proof of Theorem 1.5(a)). Fix such a $K$. By Lemma 4.6,
$V^*\Pi_iV$ and $V^*\Pi_jV$ project onto $\mathbb C^{k_c}\otimes e_i$ and $\mathbb C^{k_c}\otimes e_j$, and for
$V^*YV=\bigoplus_d1\otimes y_d$, $\Pi_iY\Pi_j=0$ iff $\langle e_i,y_ce_j\rangle=0$. The map
$Y\mapsto\langle e_i,y_ce_j\rangle$ is real-linear $\mathcal A'\to\mathbb C$ and nonzero (its value at
$V(1\otimes|e_i\rangle\langle e_j|)V^*$ is $1$), so its kernel is a null set. As $Y$ is independent of $K$,
Tonelli's theorem gives probability $0$. $\square$

**Lemma 4.8 (aligning the copies).** Let $W_i,W_j$ be invariant subspaces on which $\mathcal A$ acts
irreducibly, with orthonormal bases $Q_i,Q_j$ and restrictions $\sigma_i(a)=Q_i^*aQ_i$, and let $Y\in\mathcal A'$.
Then $T=Q_i^*YQ_j\in\operatorname{Hom}(\sigma_j,\sigma_i)$. If $T\neq0$, then $T=\lambda U$ with $\lambda>0$, $U$
unitary, and $Q_j'=Q_jU^*$ satisfies $Q_j'^*aQ_j'=\sigma_i(a)$ for all $a\in\mathcal A$. If $W_i,W_j$ lie in
different summands ($W_i\subseteq P_c\mathbb C^n$, $W_j\subseteq P_d\mathbb C^n$, $c\neq d$), then $T=0$.

*Proof.* As in the proof of Theorem 4.3, $aQ_j=Q_j\sigma_j(a)$ and $Q_i^*a=\sigma_i(a)Q_i^*$, so
$T\sigma_j(a)=Q_i^*YaQ_j=Q_i^*aYQ_j=\sigma_i(a)T$. Lemma 4.2 gives $T=\lambda U$ and
$U\sigma_j(a)U^*=\sigma_i(a)$, which is $Q_j'^*aQ_j'$. Finally $P_c\in\mathcal A\cap\mathcal A'$, $Q_i=P_cQ_i$ and
$P_cQ_j=0$, so $T=Q_i^*P_cYQ_j=Q_i^*YP_cQ_j=0$. $\square$

The algorithm (`decompose.wedderburn`, with the front ends `from_group` and `from_generators`) is now:

1. *Sample the commutant.* For all elements of a finite group, $Y=P(Z)$ and $K=P(Z+Z^*)$ with the twirl
   $P$ and Gaussian matrices $Z$: images of Gaussian laws under linear surjections onto $\mathcal A'$ and
   $\mathcal A'_h$, hence with densities. For generators, $Y,K$ are Gaussian combinations of a basis of
   $\mathcal A'=\{X:[S_j,X]=[S_j^*,X]=0\}$, the null space of a linear map of $M_n$.
2. *Split.* The eigenspaces of $K$ are irreducible (Lemma 4.6, Proposition 4.7(a)); their dimensions
   are the $k_c$.
3. *Group and align.* The blocks $Q_i^*YQ_j$ are nonzero exactly within a summand (Proposition 4.7(b),
   Lemma 4.8); the classes give the sectors, their sizes the $m_c$, and the polar factors align the copies.
4. *Assemble* $V$ in Kronecker order, so that $V^*aV=\bigoplus_ca_c\otimes1_{m_c}$.

In floating point, close eigenvalues of different copies are kept together and split by the compression
of a fresh random element of the commutant (module docstring of `decompose`). In any case the result is
certified at the end:

**Proposition 4.9 (certificate).** Let $\mathcal A$ be generated by $S_1,\dots,S_J$ and their adjoints, and
$\mathcal B=V(\bigoplus_cM_{k_c}\otimes1_{m_c})V^*$ with $V$ unitary. If all $S_j\in\mathcal B$ and
$\dim\mathcal A'=\sum_cm_c^2$, then $\mathcal A=\mathcal B$.

*Proof.* $\mathcal B$ is a $*$-algebra containing all $S_j,S_j^*$, so $\mathcal A\subseteq\mathcal B$ and
$\mathcal B'\subseteq\mathcal A'$. Theorem 4.3, applied to the representation of $\mathcal B$ on $\mathbb C^n$ (the block
maps $b\mapsto b_c$ are irreducible, and inequivalent, as the $c$-th central projection is mapped to $1$ by one
and to $0$ by the others), gives $\dim\mathcal B'=\sum_cm_c^2=\dim\mathcal A'$. So $\mathcal B'=\mathcal A'$ and
$\mathcal B=\mathcal B''=\mathcal A''=\mathcal A$. $\square$

`Decomposition.verify()` reports the relative distances of the generators (and of fresh elements of the
commutant) from $\mathcal B$ (resp. $\mathcal B'$), and compares $\sum_cm_c^2$ with $\dim\mathcal A'$ (from the
characters for a group, Corollary 4.5(c); from the null space for generators). We run it on $S_3$ and three
qubits, and then look inside: the eigenspaces of one random $K$ and the intertwiners of one random $Y$.

```python
D = dc.from_group(G)                                     # the twirl samples pi(S_3)'
print(D)
assert D.k_factors == (1, 2) and D.multiplicities == (4, 2)            # Section 2.4: k = (1, 2), m = (4, 2)
assert D.verify()['ok']
rho = D.to_algebra(G).matrix                             # (6, 2, 2, 2): the blocks rho_c(sigma), padded
assert close(rho[:, 0, 0, 0], torch.ones(6))                           # sector 1: trivial representation
assert close(rho[:, 1].diagonal(dim1=-2, dim2=-1).sum(-1), chis[2])    # sector 2: the standard one
schur = torch.einsum('gij,gkl->ijkl', rho[:, 1], rho[:, 1].conj()) / 6
assert close(schur.reshape(4, 4), torch.eye(4) / 2)                    # Theorem 4.4 with k = 2
assert close(D.from_algebra(D.to_algebra(G)), G)

Z = torch.randn(8, 8, dtype=dt)
```

## 4.4 $U(1)$ and charge sectors

**Proposition 4.10.** Let $Q$ be a Hermitian matrix on $\mathbb C^n$ with integer eigenvalues $q$ (the
**charges**), spectral projections $P_q$ and $d_q=\operatorname{rank}P_q$, and $U_\theta=e^{i\theta Q}$; we write
$U(1)'=\{U_\theta:\theta\in\mathbb R\}'$ and $U(1)''$ for its commutant.

1. $\theta\mapsto U_\theta$ is a unitary representation of $U(1)=\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z$, and every continuous
   unitary representation of $U(1)$ on $\mathbb C^n$ has this form.
2. For $X\in M_n$ the following are equivalent: (i) $XU_\theta=U_\theta X$ for all $\theta$; (ii) $[X,Q]=0$;
   (iii) $X=\sum_qP_qXP_q$. Hence $U(1)'\cong\bigoplus_qM_{d_q}(\mathbb C)$ (the block-diagonal operators) and
   $U(1)''=\operatorname{span}\{P_q\}$: all $k_c=1$, and the multiplicities are the $d_q$.
3. (*Conserved charge.*) For a Hermitian $H$, $e^{itH}Qe^{-itH}=Q$ for all $t$ iff $[H,Q]=0$, i.e. iff
   $H\in U(1)'$. Then every sector weight $\operatorname{Tr}(P_q\rho(t))$ of $\rho(t)=e^{-itH}\rho e^{itH}$ is constant.

*Proof.* (1) $U_\theta=\sum_qe^{iq\theta}P_q$ is unitary, $U_\theta U_{\theta'}=U_{\theta+\theta'}$ (Theorem 1.5(c)) and
$U_{2\pi}=1$. Conversely, for a continuous representation, $(U_\theta)_{\theta\ge0}$ is a continuous semigroup
of matrices, so $U_\theta=e^{\theta B}$ for $\theta\ge0$ by Lemma 8.5 (proved in Chapter 8; the proof uses
only finite dimension), and for $\theta<0$, $U_\theta=U_{-\theta}^{-1}=e^{\theta B}$. Differentiating
$U_\theta^*U_\theta=1$ at $0$ gives $B^*+B=0$, so $B=iQ$ with $Q$ Hermitian, and $U_{2\pi}=1$ forces
$e^{2\pi iq}=1$, $q\in\mathbb Z$, for every eigenvalue. (2) (i)$\Rightarrow$(ii): differentiate at $\theta=0$.
(ii)$\Rightarrow$(iii): $X$ commutes with $P_q=f(Q)$ (Theorem 1.5(d)), so $P_qXP_{q'}=XP_qP_{q'}=0$ for
$q\neq q'$. (iii)$\Rightarrow$(i): $XU_\theta=\sum_qe^{iq\theta}P_qXP_q=U_\theta X$. The block-diagonal operators form
$\bigoplus_qM_{d_q}\otimes1_1$, whose commutant is $\bigoplus_q1_{d_q}\otimes M_1=\operatorname{span}\{P_q\}$ (Theorem 2.6).
(3) $\frac d{dt}e^{itH}Qe^{-itH}=ie^{itH}[H,Q]e^{-itH}$ (Proposition 8.2) vanishes for all $t$ iff $[H,Q]=0$.
Then $e^{itH}$ commutes with $P_q$ (Theorem 1.5(d), twice), and
$\operatorname{Tr}(P_q\rho(t))=\operatorname{Tr}(e^{itH}P_qe^{-itH}\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}(P_q\rho)$. $\square$

The irreducible representations of $U(1)$ are thus the characters $\theta\mapsto e^{iq\theta}$, $q\in\mathbb Z$ (one-
dimensional, as for every abelian group), and the multiplicity of the charge $q$ is $d_q$. Charges add
under tensor products: $e^{i\theta(Q_1\otimes1+1\otimes Q_2)}=e^{i\theta Q_1}\otimes e^{i\theta Q_2}$ acts on the range of
$P_{q_1}\otimes P_{q_2}$ with charge $q_1+q_2$. This is the fusion rule $(q_1,q_2)\mapsto q_1+q_2$ of the fused
tensor products of Chapter 7.

For $n$ qubits the charge is the **number operator** $\hat N=\sum_jn_j$, $n_j=|1\rangle\langle1|$ on qubit
$j$: $\hat N|b\rangle=|b|\,|b\rangle$ with the Hamming weight $|b|$. Its sectors have dimension
$\binom nw$, so the operators that conserve the Hamming weight form
$$
U(1)'\cong\bigoplus_{w=0}^nM_{\binom nw}(\mathbb C),
$$
which is `chains.SpinChain(n).algebra`, the fused product of $n$ copies of $M_1\oplus M_1$ with charges $0,1$
(bit $1$ = spin up, $\hat N=S^z+\frac n2$; in `decompose.total_spin` spin up is instead the *first* basis
vector of $\mathbb C^2$, which relabels the basis but not the sectors). Every XXZ Hamiltonian
$\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}J(s^x_is^x_j+s^y_is^y_j+\Delta s^z_is^z_j)$ conserves $\hat N$, because the hopping term
$s^x_is^x_j+s^y_is^y_j=\frac12(s^+_is^-_j+s^-_is^+_j)$ moves an up spin from one site to another.

```python
from torch_vn_algebra import SpinChain

L = 4
Sx, Sy, Sz = dc.total_spin(L)                              # (16, 16) each, site 0 most significant
N_op = Sz + L / 2 * torch.eye(2 ** L, dtype=dt)            # number of up spins
DU = dc.from_generators(N_op)                              # the algebra generated by U(1)
print(DU)
assert set(DU.k_factors) == {1}
assert sorted(DU.multiplicities) == sorted(math.comb(L, w) for w in range(L + 1))
chain = SpinChain(L, boundary='periodic')
assert sorted(DU.commutant_algebra().k_factors) == sorted(chain.algebra.k_factors)

sx, sy, sz = dc.spin_matrices(0.5)
def two_site(a, b, i, j, n=L):                             # a on site i, b on site j of n sites
    ops = [torch.eye(2, dtype=dt)] * n
    ops[i], ops[j] = a, b
    out = ops[0]
    for o in ops[1:]:
        out = torch.kron(out, o)
    return out

H = sum(two_site(sx, sx, i, j) + two_site(sy, sy, i, j) + 0.5 * two_site(sz, sz, i, j)
        for i, j in chain.bonds)                           # XXZ ring, Delta = 0.5
assert close(H @ N_op, N_op @ H)                           # [H, N] = 0 ...
assert close(DU.from_commutant(DU.to_commutant(H)), H)     # ... so H is block diagonal (Proposition 4.10)
Hc = chain.xxz(J=1.0, Delta=0.5)                           # the same H, built sector by sector
E_sec = torch.cat([torch.linalg.eigvalsh(Hc.matrix[0, N, :k, :k]) for N, k in enumerate(chain.algebra.k_factors)])
assert close(torch.sort(E_sec).values, torch.linalg.eigvalsh(H))
```

> **Physical meaning.** $U(1)$ symmetry is the conservation of a particle number, a charge or a
> magnetisation. Proposition 4.10(3) is Noether's theorem in this setting: the charge is conserved iff the
> Hamiltonian is symmetric, and then the dynamics never leaves a sector (Theorem 8.3). If in addition
> nothing can measure coherences between sectors, the sectors are superselection sectors (Chapter 3).

## 4.5 $SU(2)$: spin

**Definition.** A **spin representation** on $\mathbb C^d$ is a triple of Hermitian matrices
$S_x,S_y,S_z$ with
$$
[S_x,S_y]=iS_z,\qquad[S_y,S_z]=iS_x,\qquad[S_z,S_x]=iS_y,
$$
i.e. a representation of the labels $\{x,y,z\}$ (Section 4.1) with these relations. The basic example is
$s_a=\frac12\sigma_a$ on $\mathbb C^2$ (Pauli matrices). If $(S_a)$ and $(T_a)$ are spin representations, so is
$S_a\otimes1+1\otimes T_a$ (the cross terms commute); hence the **total spin** $S_a=\sum_is_a^{(i)}$ of $L$
spins $\frac12$ (`decompose.total_spin(L)`) is one. The rotations $e^{-i\theta\,\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf S}$ commute
with $X$ for all $\theta,\mathbf n$ iff $X$ commutes with the three $S_a$ (differentiate at $\theta=0$; conversely
Theorem 1.5(d)), so the commutant of "the $SU(2)$ action" is $\{S_x,S_y,S_z\}'$, and by Theorem 2.2 the
rotations and the $S_a$ generate the same algebra. For $L$ spins $\frac12$ the rotations are the $V^{\otimes L}$,
$V\in SU(2)$: every such $V$ is some $e^{-i\theta\,\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s}$, and
$e^{-i\theta\,\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf S}=(e^{-i\theta\,\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf s})^{\otimes L}$.

Put $S_\pm=S_x\pm iS_y$, so $S_-=S_+^*$, and $\mathbf S^2=S_x^2+S_y^2+S_z^2$. The relations give
$$
[S_z,S_\pm]=\pm S_\pm,\qquad[S_+,S_-]=2S_z,\qquad\mathbf S^2=S_-S_++S_z^2+S_z,
$$
and $\mathbf S^2$ commutes with every $S_a$ (e.g.
$[\mathbf S^2,S_z]=S_x[S_x,S_z]+[S_x,S_z]S_x+S_y[S_y,S_z]+[S_y,S_z]S_y=-i\{S_x,S_y\}+i\{S_y,S_x\}=0$).

**Theorem 4.11 (irreducible spin representations).** For every $s\in\{0,\frac12,1,\frac32,\dots\}$ the
matrices of `decompose.spin_matrices(s)` — on $\mathbb C^{2s+1}$ with basis $|m\rangle$, $m=s,s-1,\dots,-s$,
$$
S_z|m\rangle=m|m\rangle,\qquad S_+|m\rangle=\sqrt{s(s+1)-m(m+1)}\;|m+1\rangle,\qquad S_-=S_+^*,
$$
form an irreducible spin representation (**spin $s$**) with $\mathbf S^2=s(s+1)1$. Every irreducible spin
representation is unitarily equivalent to exactly one of them.

*Proof.* *Spin $s$ is a spin representation.* $[S_z,S_+]=S_+$ is clear. $S_+S_-|m\rangle=(s(s+1)-(m-1)m)|m\rangle$
and $S_-S_+|m\rangle=(s(s+1)-m(m+1))|m\rangle$, so $[S_+,S_-]=2S_z$; these two relations and their adjoints are
equivalent to the three defining ones. $\mathbf S^2|m\rangle=(s(s+1)-m(m+1)+m^2+m)|m\rangle=s(s+1)|m\rangle$.
*Irreducible:* an invariant $W\neq0$ is invariant under $S_z$, whose eigenvalues are simple, so it contains
some $|m\rangle$; the coefficients of $S_\pm$ vanish only at $m=\pm s$, so applying $S_\pm$ gives all $|m'\rangle$.

*Classification.* Let $(S_a)$ be irreducible on $\mathbb C^d$, $\lambda$ the largest eigenvalue of $S_z$ and
$v_0$ a unit eigenvector. $S_zS_+v_0=S_+(S_z+1)v_0=(\lambda+1)S_+v_0$, so $S_+v_0=0$ by maximality. Let
$v_j=S_-^jv_0$; then $S_zv_j=(\lambda-j)v_j$, and by induction, using $S_+S_-=S_-S_++2S_z$,
$$
S_+v_j=j(2\lambda-j+1)\,v_{j-1}
$$
($S_+v_j=S_-S_+v_{j-1}+2S_zv_{j-1}=[(j-1)(2\lambda-j+2)+2(\lambda-j+1)]v_{j-1}$). Nonzero $v_j$ are eigenvectors of
$S_z$ for distinct eigenvalues, so there is a last nonzero one, $v_r$. The span of $v_0,\dots,v_r$ is invariant
under $S_z,S_\pm$, hence equals $\mathbb C^d$, and $d=r+1$. From $0=S_+v_{r+1}=(r+1)(2\lambda-r)v_r$,
$\lambda=r/2=:s$. Finally $\|v_j\|^2=\langle v_{j-1},S_+v_j\rangle=j(2s-j+1)\|v_{j-1}\|^2$, so the unit vectors
$|s-j\rangle=v_j/\|v_j\|$ satisfy $S_+|m\rangle=\sqrt{(s-m)(s+m+1)}\,|m+1\rangle$ with $m=s-j$, which is the formula
above. Different $s$ have different dimensions. $\square$

**Corollary 4.12.** Every spin representation on $\mathbb C^n$ is unitarily equivalent to
$\bigoplus_s(\text{spin }s)\otimes1_{m_s}$, and with $d_M=\dim\ker(S_z-M)$,
$$
m_s=d_s-d_{s+1}.
$$

*Proof.* Theorem 4.3 and Theorem 4.11. Each copy of spin $s'$ contains exactly one eigenvector of $S_z$ for
the eigenvalue $M$ if $|M|\le s'$ and $s'-M\in\mathbb Z$, and none otherwise; so
$d_M=\sum_{s'\ge|M|,\,s'-M\in\mathbb Z}m_{s'}$, and the difference of two consecutive values is $m_s$. $\square$

**Proposition 4.13 ($L$ spins $\frac12$).** The total spin on $(\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes L}$ contains spin $S$ iff
$S\in\{\frac L2,\frac L2-1,\dots\}$, $S\ge0$, with multiplicity
$$
m_S=\binom L{\frac L2-S}-\binom L{\frac L2-S-1}\qquad\Big(\tbinom L{-1}=0\Big),
$$
so the algebra generated by $S_x,S_y,S_z$ is $\bigoplus_SM_{2S+1}\otimes1_{m_S}$ and its commutant
$\bigoplus_S1_{2S+1}\otimes M_{m_S}$.

*Proof.* $S_z|b\rangle=(|b|-\frac L2)|b\rangle$, so $d_M=\binom L{L/2+M}=\binom L{L/2-M}$ if $\frac L2+M\in\{0,\dots,L\}$ and $0$
otherwise. Apply Corollary 4.12 and Theorem 4.3. The values are positive because
$\binom L{j+1}/\binom Lj=\frac{L-j}{j+1}>1$ for $j<\frac{L-1}2$. $\square$

For $L=2$: $m_1=m_0=1$, so $\frac12\otimes\frac12=1\oplus0$ (**Clebsch–Gordan**): the triplet $|{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle$,
$\frac1{\sqrt2}(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle+|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle)=\frac1{\sqrt2}S_-|{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle$,
$|{\downarrow\downarrow}\rangle$, which is symmetric under the swap $F$ of the two spins, and the singlet
$\frac1{\sqrt2}(|{\uparrow\downarrow}\rangle-|{\downarrow\uparrow}\rangle)$, annihilated by $S_+$ and $S_z$ and
antisymmetric. For $L=4$: $m_2=1$, $m_1=4-1=3$, $m_0=6-4=2$, so
$$
\{S_x,S_y,S_z\}''\cong M_5\oplus M_3\oplus M_1\ \text{ with multiplicities }1,3,2,\qquad
\{S_x,S_y,S_z\}'\cong M_1\oplus M_3\oplus M_2,
$$
of dimensions $35$ and $14$ — the numbers found in Chapter 10. The library finds the same from the three
generators alone (sectors sorted by $(k_c,m_c)$):


Which operators commute with all rotations? The permutations of the sites do: $\pi(\sigma)$ maps
$s^{(i)}_a$ to $s^{(\sigma(i))}_a$ by conjugation, so it fixes $\sum_is^{(i)}_a$. The converse is a classical
theorem.

**Theorem 4.14 (Schur–Weyl duality).** Let $\pi$ be the representation of $S_L$ permuting the factors of
$(\mathbb C^2)^{\otimes L}$. (a) $\operatorname{span}\pi(S_L)\subseteq\{S_x,S_y,S_z\}'$. (b) Equality holds;
equivalently (Theorem 2.2), the algebra generated by the total spin is $\pi(S_L)'$.

Part (a) was just proved. Part (b) is stated without proof for general $L$; it holds for
$(\mathbb C^d)^{\otimes L}$ and the diagonal action of $U(d)$ (W. Fulton and J. Harris, *Representation
Theory*, Springer 1991, Lecture 6; R. Goodman and N. Wallach, *Symmetry, Representations, and Invariants*,
Springer 2009, Section 9.1). For $L\le5$ we check it: by (a) it suffices that the two spaces have the same
dimension. For $L=2$ it is Lemma 5.8 with $k=2$, since $\{S_a\}'=\{V\otimes V:V\in SU(2)\}'=\{V\otimes V:V\in U(2)\}'$
(a phase acts as a scalar).

```python
for n in range(2, 6):

    dims = [torch.linalg.matrix_rank(A).item() for A in (comm, perm, torch.cat([comm, perm]))]
    print(n, dims)
    assert dims[0] == dims[1] == dims[2]                                     # span pi(S_n) = {S_a}'
```

The dimensions printed are $2,5,14,42$, the Catalan numbers $\binom{2L}L/(L+1)$. The last line is duality in
action: $\pi(S_4)''$ has blocks $k=(1,2,3)$ with multiplicities $(5,1,3)$ — the pairs $(k_c,m_c)$ of the total
spin with the roles exchanged. The multiplicity space of spin $S$ carries an irreducible representation of
$S_L$, here of dimensions $1,3,2$ for $S=2,1,0$.

> **Physical meaning.** A spin-$S$ multiplet consists of $2S+1$ states of equal energy for every
> rotation-invariant Hamiltonian, distinguished by $S^z=M$. Two spins $\frac12$ form a triplet and a singlet;
> four spins form one quintet, three triplets and two singlets. The multiplicity spaces of the two
> singlets and of the three triplets are the decoherence-free qubit and the noiseless qutrit of Chapter 10.

## 4.6 Spin systems

The **Heisenberg model** on $L$ spins $\frac12$ is $H=\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}\,\mathbf s^{(i)}\cdot\mathbf s^{(j)}$ with
real couplings $J_{ij}$.

**Proposition 4.15.** (a) (*Dirac's identity*) $\mathbf s^{(i)}\cdot\mathbf s^{(j)}=\frac12F_{ij}-\frac14$, where $F_{ij}$
swaps the spins $i$ and $j$. Hence every Heisenberg Hamiltonian lies in $\operatorname{span}\pi(S_L)\subseteq\{S_a\}'$,
and $V^*HV=\bigoplus_S1_{2S+1}\otimes H_S$ with blocks $H_S\in M_{m_S}$ in the decomposition of
Proposition 4.13: each eigenvalue of $H_S$ is a $(2S+1)$-fold degenerate level of $H$.
(b) The space of **highest-weight vectors** $\ker S_+\cap\ker(S_z-S)$ has dimension $m_S$, is invariant under
$H$, and $H$ acts on it as $H_S$ (up to a unitary change of basis).

*Proof.* (a) On two spins, $\mathbf s^{(1)}\cdot\mathbf s^{(2)}=\frac12(\mathbf S^2-\mathbf s^{(1)2}-\mathbf s^{(2)2})=\frac12\mathbf S^2-\frac34$
(Theorem 4.11 with $s=\frac12$). On the triplet, $\mathbf S^2=2$ and $F=1$ (it is spanned by
$S_-^j|{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle$, and $F$ commutes with $S_-$ and fixes $|{\uparrow\uparrow}\rangle$); on the singlet,
$\mathbf S^2=0$ and $F=-1$. Both sides agree on these four vectors. For sites $i,j$ of $L$ tensor with the
identity. The rest is Theorem 4.14(a) and Theorem 4.3. (b) In the decomposition of Corollary 4.12, a vector
with $S_z=S$ is $\sum_{S'\ge S}|S',S\rangle\otimes w_{S'}$, and $S_+|S',S\rangle\neq0$ for $S'>S$ by Theorem 4.11; so
$\ker S_+\cap\ker(S_z-S)=|S,S\rangle\otimes\mathbb C^{m_S}$, on which $1\otimes H_S$ acts as $H_S$. $\square$

Part (b) is how the module `su2` (spin chains, Chapter 9) builds $H_S$ without any numerical decomposition:
an orthonormal basis of the highest-weight vectors is constructed by Clebsch–Gordan coupling one site at
a time, inside the $U(1)$ sector $\hat N=\frac L2+S$ of `SpinChain`. We check (a) on a ring of four spins
with the decomposition `D4` found above, and then compare the block sizes for eight spins.


> **Physical meaning.** The Heisenberg model describes the exchange interaction of localised electron
> spins in magnetic insulators; $J_{ij}>0$ favours antiparallel neighbours. Its spectrum consists of
> multiplets, and a magnetic field $h\,S^z$ splits each multiplet into $2S+1$ equidistant levels (Exercise 4).
> For couplings $J_{ij}>0$ between the two sublattices of a bipartite lattice, $J_{ij}\le0$ within them,
> and sublattices of equal size, the ground state is a singlet (Lieb–Mattis theorem, E. Lieb and D. Mattis,
> *J. Math. Phys.* 3 (1962) 749; stated without proof). The ring of eight spins above is an example.

## 4.7 What to compare with

For finite groups, GAP and SageMath compute character tables exactly, and Corollary 4.5 then gives the
multiplicities; RepLAB (MATLAB; D. Rosset, F. Montealegre-Mora, J.-D. Bancal) decomposes representations
of finite and compact groups numerically, also with random elements of the commutant, and
SymbolicWedderburn.jl (Julia) builds exact Wedderburn decompositions for
symmetry reduction of semidefinite programs. In physics, QuSpin and the symmetry modules of
exact-diagonalisation codes block-diagonalise $U(1)$ (and lattice) symmetries, ITensor and TeNPy carry
$U(1)$ charges in tensor networks, and TensorKit.jl handles $SU(2)$ with fusion trees. The library's
`decompose` is a dense numerical method for small matrices ($n$ up to a few hundred); what it adds is
that it accepts any list of matrices — group elements or generators, no group structure required —,
certifies its result (Proposition 4.9), and returns the algebra and its commutant as `TypeIAlgebra`
objects with `to_algebra`, `from_algebra`, `to_commutant` and `from_commutant`, so that everything in
Chapters 3, 5–8 applies at once. For spin-$\frac12$ chains, `su2.SU2Chain` gives the $SU(2)$ blocks of the
previous section directly, up to $L=16$.

2. Let $j_1\ge j_2$. $S_z$ has the eigenvalue $m_1+m_2$ on $|m_1\rangle\otimes|m_2\rangle$, so for

```python
def coupled(a, b):                                        # S^(a) (x) 1 + 1 (x) S^(b)
    Sa, Sb = dc.spin_matrices(a), dc.spin_matrices(b)
    ea, eb = torch.eye(Sa.shape[-1], dtype=dt), torch.eye(Sb.shape[-1], dtype=dt)
    return torch.stack([torch.kron(x, eb) + torch.kron(ea, y) for x, y in zip(Sa, Sb)])
```
