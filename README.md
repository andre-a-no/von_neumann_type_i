**torch_vn_algebra** is a Python library for batched computation with block-diagonal matrices in symmetry sectors,
the finite-dimensional Type I von Neumann algebras ⊕ M_k(ℂ), in PyTorch on CPU and GPU. It provides random
operators and Haar-random unitaries, density matrices, quantum channels (Kraus, Choi, Stinespring), Schrödinger and
Lindblad dynamics, and spin chains in U(1), momentum and SU(2) sectors; it computes the Wedderburn (block-diagonal)
decomposition and the commutant of the algebra generated by given matrices or by a group representation, conditional
expectations and Jones indices, Jordan algebras and quantum Rényi divergences. Operators, states and channels are
batched over thousands of samples, differentiable with autograd, and available in single or double precision.

It is written for physicists (superselection and charge sectors, exact diagonalisation, open quantum systems),
quantum-information researchers (states, channels, entropies, error correction), mathematicians (operator algebras,
random matrices, representation theory) and machine-learning researchers (equivariant linear layers as commutants).
The theory behind every function is in self-contained [lecture notes](man/lecture_notes/README.md) with complete
proofs, and the library is shown at work in a [tutorial](man/tutorial/README.md) whose code is run by the tests, both
in English, Spanish, French, German, Chinese, Japanese and Russian; documentation website:
<https://andre-a-no.github.io/von_neumann_type_i/>.

The algebras are direct sums of full matrix algebras:

```math
\mathcal M = \bigoplus_{c=1}^{C} M_{k_c}(\mathbb C)\quad\text{acting on}\quad \mathcal H=\bigoplus_{c=1}^{C}\mathbb C^{k_c}.
```

(Nominal factor sizes `n_factors` $`n_c\ge k_c`$ describe computations in the corner $`p\mathcal M_0p`$ of a larger
algebra $`\mathcal M_0=\bigoplus_c M_{n_c}`$ with rank-$`k_c`$ projections $`p_c`$; everything refers to the corner.)

Operators are stored as one tensor of shape `(batch, C, k_max, k_max)`: the batch axis holds
samples (or optimisation starts), the sector axis holds the direct summands, and block `c` occupies the
top-left `k_c × k_c` corner (the rest is zero padding).

Companion paper: I. Nikolaeva, A. Novikov, *Finite-Dimensional Type I von Neumann Algebras in
PyTorch: A GPU-Accelerated Framework for Random Block-Diagonal Operators*, arXiv:2606.15882
(source and PDF in [`man/article/`](man/article)).

## Features

- **Block-diagonal operators in batches** – an operator of ⊕_c M_{k_c} is one tensor `(batch, C, k_max, k_max)`;
  products, adjoints, traces, norms and functional calculus run as batched kernels on CPU or CUDA GPU
  ([Tutorial 1](man/tutorial/01_matrix_algebras.md), [2.5](man/tutorial/02_von_neumann_algebras.md#s-2-5)).
- **Random matrices and Haar-random unitaries** – Haar measure on U(n), O(n), SU(n), SO(n), COE, CSE, random
  operators with a prescribed spectrum, GUE/GOE level statistics, Weingarten-calculus checks
  ([Tutorial 5](man/tutorial/05_random_matrices.md)).
- **Quantum states and entropy** – density matrices (Hilbert–Schmidt, Bures, fixed rank), Gibbs states, von Neumann
  entropy, relative entropy, fidelity, trace distance ([Tutorial 3](man/tutorial/03_traces_and_states.md)).
- **Quantum channels** – Kraus operators, Choi matrix, Stinespring dilation, superoperator; dephasing, depolarizing,
  amplitude damping, random channels, channels between sectors ([Tutorial 6](man/tutorial/06_quantum_channels.md)).
- **Tensor products and entanglement** – partial trace, fusion of U(1) charges, entanglement entropy, Page's formula
  ([Tutorial 7](man/tutorial/07_tensor_products.md)).
- **Schrödinger and Lindblad (GKSL) master equations** – exact propagators, RK4, time steps that are CPTP channels,
  quantum jumps between sectors, batched Hamiltonians ([Tutorial 8](man/tutorial/08_dynamics.md)).
- **Spin chains and exact diagonalisation in symmetry sectors** – XXZ and Heisenberg chains in U(1) magnetisation,
  momentum and SU(2) sectors, Lanczos ground states, Krylov time evolution, disorder averages, level-spacing ratio
  ([Tutorial 9](man/tutorial/09_spin_chains.md)).
- **Wedderburn decomposition, commutants and group representations** – block diagonalisation of the matrix
  *-algebra generated by matrices, a Lie algebra or a finite group (isotypic decomposition, multiplicities,
  Schur–Weyl duality), real/complex/quaternionic type, Clebsch–Gordan coefficients and the Wigner–Eckart theorem
  ([Tutorial 4](man/tutorial/04_symmetries.md), [Appendix B](man/tutorial/B_schur_weyl.md)).
- **Quantum error correction** – noiseless subsystems, decoherence-free subspaces, the Knill–Laflamme conditions,
  operator-algebra error correction ([Tutorial 10](man/tutorial/10_quantum_computing.md)).
- **Conditional expectations and inclusions of algebras** – Jones index, basic construction, Pimsner–Popa constants,
  Bratteli diagrams ([Tutorial 2.6](man/tutorial/02_von_neumann_algebras.md#s-2-6),
  [`example_inclusions.py`](examples/example_inclusions.py)).
- **Quantum Rényi divergences** – Petz and sandwiched Rényi divergences, Umegaki relative entropy, D_max, Petz recovery
  maps ([`example_divergences.py`](examples/example_divergences.py)).
- **Constrained optimisation** – batched multistart optimisation over unitaries and positive operators, trace
  inequalities ([Tutorial 11](man/tutorial/11_optimisation.md)).
- **Equivariant machine learning** – equivariant linear layers as the commutant of a group representation
  ([Tutorial 12](man/tutorial/12_equivariant_learning.md)).
- **Jordan algebras** – Jordan product, quadratic representation, spin factors and the Lorentz cone, the Jordan
  algebra generated by matrices ([Tutorial 13](man/tutorial/13_jordan_algebras.md)).

## When to use it, and alternatives

torch_vn_algebra pays off when the block structure is part of the problem: many sectors of different sizes
(superselection or charge sectors, isotypic components of a symmetry), many samples or optimisation starts at once,
and quantities that are defined by the algebra (its traces, states, centre, conditional expectations, channels between
sectors). For one dense matrix at a time, for very large sparse systems, or for tensor networks, the established tools
below are the better choice; the sections *What to compare with* of the tutorial compare them in detail.

| task | established tools | what torch_vn_algebra adds |
|---|---|---|
| open quantum systems ([8.6](man/tutorial/08_dynamics.md#s-8-6)) | QuTiP (`mesolve`, `mcsolve`), dynamiqs (JAX, GPU), QuantumOptics.jl | Lindblad dynamics sector by sector, jumps between sectors, batches of Hamiltonians; no adaptive step size and no quantum trajectories |
| exact diagonalisation ([9.7](man/tutorial/09_spin_chains.md#s-9-7)) | QuSpin, XDiag, SPINPACK, HΦ | the chain as an algebra, so states, channels and entropies apply per sector; SU(2) blocks; batched disorder; dense to L ≈ 16, Krylov to L ≈ 24 |
| symmetric tensor networks ([0.3](man/tutorial/00_overview.md#s-0-3)) | ITensor, TeNPy, TensorKit.jl, YASTN | no tensor networks; instead traces, states, conditional expectations and Haar measures of the algebra |
| quantum channels ([6.6](man/tutorial/06_quantum_channels.md#s-6-6)) | Qiskit `quantum_info`, toqito, QETLAB, QuTiP | Kraus operators stored per sector and in batches, maps between different algebras, duals for each trace |
| random unitaries ([5.5](man/tutorial/05_random_matrices.md#s-5-5)) | `scipy.stats.unitary_group`, QuTiP `rand_unitary` | batched Haar sampling on the product of unitary groups U(k_1) × … × U(k_C), on GPU |
| representations, Wedderburn decomposition ([4.8](man/tutorial/04_symmetries.md#s-4-8)) | GAP, SageMath, RepLAB (MATLAB), SymbolicWedderburn.jl | a dense numerical decomposition with a certificate, returned as an algebra to compute in |
| equivariant neural networks ([12.5](man/tutorial/12_equivariant_learning.md#s-12-5)) | escnn, e3nn, EMLP | the commutant as a *-algebra ⊕ M_{m_c}: products, positivity and functional calculus blockwise and differentiable; dense, small n only |

## Features in detail

- **Random operators with any spectrum** – `operator_from_eigenvalues(sampler, ...)` builds
  `A_c = U_c diag(λ) U_c*` from a user callable `sampler(dim) -> (dim,) or (batch, dim)`.
- **Unitary ensembles** – Haar on U(n)/O(n), SU(n)/SO(n), COE, CSE, random diagonal phases;
  batched (`random_unitary(n, measure, batch_size)`), and block-diagonal unitaries in the algebra
  (`random_unitary_operator`).
- **Lazy evaluation** – `X @ Y`, `X + Y`, `X * s` and functional calculus build a recipe that is computed when
  `.matrix` (or a scalar functional) is requested; methods that need a property of the base operator first
  (e.g. `X.inv`, `X.sqrt()` checking positivity) evaluate the base.
- **Functional calculus at four levels** (module `functional`) – `apply_function(f)` for self-adjoint and for normal
  operators (unitaries; via the Hermitian eigensolver of H + αK), `abs`, `inverse` (pseudo-inverse with a relative
  cutoff), `polar`, norms and singular values (on GPUs for blocks > 32 via the Hermitian eigensolver of
  [[0, A], [A*, 0]], since batched SVD stops at 32), `holomorphic_function(f)` for any operator (contour integral,
  batched LU solves, error estimate); `sqrt`, `log`, `power`, `expm`, `entropy`; `eigenvalues()` without
  eigenvectors. Gradients are finite at degenerate spectra (Daleckii–Krein divided differences). Opt-in cache of the
  eigendecomposition: `A.cache_spectrum()` or `with torch_vn_algebra.caching(): ...`.
- **Three trace functionals** – `Tr_blunt` (Σ_c Tr A_c), `Tr_norm` (Σ_c Tr A_c / k_c) and the
  tracial state `tau_vN` ((1/C) Σ_c Tr A_c / k_c); norms `trace_norm`, `frobenius_norm`,
  `operator_norm`; `michelson_contrast`.
- **Hilbert space utilities** – `HilbertSpace` with inner products, orthonormal bases
  (standard / random / Haar), embedding and restriction of vectors and operators.
- **Inclusions of algebras** (module `inclusions`) – `Inclusion(N, M, Lambda)` for N ⊂ M given by an inclusion matrix
  (and an optional batched unitary): the trace-preserving conditional expectation for any faithful trace, Markov
  trace and index ‖Λ‖², Watatani index, Pimsner–Popa constants, basic construction, Jones projection and tower,
  relative commutant, Bratteli diagrams, and the U(1) spin-chain tower A_L ⊂ A_{L+1} (index 4cos²(π/(2L+4))).
- **Wedderburn decomposition** (module `decompose`) – the von Neumann algebra generated by given matrices or by a
  finite group, V\*AV = ⊕_c M_{k_c} ⊗ 1_{m_c}, by the randomised algorithm of Murota et al. (2010), verified before it
  is returned; maps between n×n matrices and operators of ⊕_c M_{k_c} and of the commutant ⊕_c M_{m_c}.
- **SU(2) symmetry of spin-1/2 chains** (module `su2`) – `SU2Chain`: the algebra ⊕_S M_{m_S} of SU(2)-invariant
  operators, orthonormal highest-weight (Yamanouchi) bases in the U(1) sectors of `SpinChain`, Heisenberg Hamiltonians
  with J₂ and fields, full spectra with quantum numbers, exact Clebsch–Gordan coefficients, Wigner–Eckart reduced
  matrix elements and spin correlations (L = 14: largest block 1001 instead of 3432).
- **Quantum Rényi divergences** (module `divergences`) – Petz and sandwiched Rényi divergences with exact support
  conventions, Umegaki, D_max and D_0 as limits, per-sector decomposition, Petz, rotated and universal recovery maps,
  noncommutative L^p and Kosaki norms; batched and differentiable.
- **Jordan algebras** (module `jordan`) – Jordan product, multiplication operator, quadratic representation
  P(y)x = yxy, Jordan triple product and powers, the transpose as a Jordan automorphism, the Jordan algebra generated by
  Hermitian matrices (`jordan_algebra_generated`, compared with the *-algebra they generate), spin factors V_m and the
  Lorentz cone (`SpinFactor`).
- **Permutation-invariant ensembles** (module `permutational`) – N two-level systems in the Schur–Weyl algebra
  ⊕_j M_{2j+1} ⊗ 1_{m_j}: collective spin operators, Dicke and coherent states, local emission, pumping and dephasing as
  maps between spin sectors (`local_jump`, an `InterSectorChannel`), and a batched banded Lindbladian with collective and
  local terms (`PermutationalLindbladian`, O(N³) per step; checked against the full 2^N space and QuTiP's PIQS).

## Quick start

```python
import torch
from torch_vn_algebra import TypeIAlgebra

device = 'cuda' if torch.cuda.is_available() else 'cpu'

# M = M_4(C) (+) M_6(C), acting on C^4 (+) C^6
alg = TypeIAlgebra(n_factors=[4, 6], k_factors=[4, 6], device=device)

# 100 random positive operators: eigenvalues ~ U[0, 1], Haar-random eigenvectors per block
X = alg.operator_from_eigenvalues(lambda dim: torch.rand(100, dim, device=device),
                                  batch_size=100, force_positive=True, force_self_adjoint=True)

print(X.Tr_blunt().real.mean())            # sum of block traces
print(X.Tr_norm().real.mean())             # sum of normalised block traces
print(X.tau_vN().real.mean())              # tracial state (1/C) sum_c Tr(A_c)/k_c (complex in general)
print(X.michelson_contrast.mean())         # (lmax - lmin)/(lmax + lmin), shape (100,)

# operations stay lazy until .matrix or a scalar functional is requested
U = alg.random_unitary_operator(batch_size=100)        # block-diagonal Haar unitary
Z = X.sqrt() @ U @ X.sqrt()
print(Z.trace_norm().mean(), X.entropy().mean())
```

### Channels, states and dynamics

```python
import torch
from torch_vn_algebra import TypeIAlgebra, channels, dynamics, states

alg = TypeIAlgebra([4, 4], [4, 4], complex_valued=True)          # two superselection sectors
rho = states.random_density_matrix(alg, batch_size=100)            # HS-random states on M
H = alg.operator_from_eigenvalues(lambda d: torch.randn(100, d), batch_size=100,
                                  force_self_adjoint=True)

Phi = channels.random_channel(alg, kraus_rank=2, batch_size=100)   # Haar Stinespring isometry
print(Phi.is_trace_preserving(), Phi.is_unital())
print(states.relative_entropy(Phi(rho), Phi(states.gibbs_state(H, 1.0))))

psi0 = torch.zeros(100, 2, 4, dtype=torch.complex64)               # state vectors, block layout
psi0[:, 0, 0] = 1.0
psi_t = dynamics.schrodinger(psi0, H, times=[0.0, 1.0, 2.0])       # exact, shape (3, 100, 2, 4)

N = alg.from_blocks([torch.diag(torch.arange(4.0))] * 2)           # dephasing jump operator
rho_t = dynamics.lindblad_evolve(rho, H, jumps=[N], times=[0, 1, 2])  # RK4, list of states
Lt = dynamics.lindblad_channel(alg, H, jumps=[N], t=2.0)            # exact exp(tL) as a Channel
print((Lt(rho) - rho_t[-1]).frobenius_norm().max())
```

### Inclusions, symmetries and divergences

```python
import torch
from torch_vn_algebra import TypeIAlgebra, SU2Chain, states
from torch_vn_algebra import inclusions as inc, decompose as dc, divergences as dv

# inclusion N = M_2 (+) C  c  M = M_4 (+) M_3 with inclusion matrix Lambda
N = TypeIAlgebra([2, 1], [2, 1], precision='double')
M = TypeIAlgebra([4, 3], [4, 3], precision='double')
I = inc.Inclusion(N, M, [[1, 2], [1, 1]])
E = I.conditional_expectation('markov')                  # the trace-preserving conditional expectation M -> N
y = M.operator(torch.randn(5, M.C, M.k_max, M.k_max, dtype=M.dtype))
print(E(y).algebra is N)                                 # True: E(y) is an operator of N (batch 5)
print(I.index(), E.pimsner_popa_constant())              # [M : N] = ||Lambda||^2, Pimsner-Popa constant
print([round(T.index(), 6) for T in inc.chain_tower(4)])  # spin-chain tower A_L c A_{L+1}: 4 cos^2(pi/(2L+4))

# the algebra generated by a symmetry: S_3 permuting three qubits
G = dc.permutation_representation(3, 2)                  # (6, 8, 8): all group elements
D = dc.from_group(G)
print(D.k_factors, D.multiplicities)                     # (1, 2), (4, 2): pi(S_3)'' = M_1 (+) M_2
blocks = D.to_algebra(G)                                 # the group elements as operators of D.algebra

# SU(2) blocks of the Heisenberg ring, and Renyi divergences of states on M
chain = SU2Chain(12, 'periodic', complex_valued=False)
print(chain.multiplicities, chain.ground_state(chain.heisenberg()).energy)   # [132, 297, ...], -5.387390917
rho, sigma = states.random_density_matrix(M, 10), states.random_density_matrix(M, 10)
print(dv.sandwiched_renyi_divergence(rho, sigma, 2.0), dv.petz_renyi_divergence(rho, sigma, 0.5))
```

Lazy operators are evaluated once and cached; after that they drop their recipe, so the
intermediate results of an expression such as `X @ U @ Y` can be garbage-collected. Laziness
defers and skips work but does not fuse operations; where it matters the library uses direct
formulas instead (e.g. `Tr(ρA)` in O(k²) via `states.trace_of_product`).

Channels map each sector to itself, so the Heisenberg dual is the same for `Tr_blunt`, `Tr_norm`
and `tau_vN`, and Hamiltonian / Lindblad dynamics with generators in M conserve the sector
probabilities `Tr rho_c`. Unitary dynamics requires `complex_valued=True`.

**Number field and precision** are two independent switches of every algebra:
`complex_valued` (real / complex) and `precision` (`'single'` / `'double'`), i.e. float32, float64,
complex64 or complex128; `alg.like(complex_valued=..., precision=...)` and `op.cast(alg2)` move between
them, `alg.bytes_per_operator(B)` reports the memory. Rules of thumb (details and measurements in the
paper, `scripts/numerics.py`):

| use | when |
|---|---|
| real | the problem is real: real symmetric spectra, ground states without complex couplings, GOE statistics, orthogonal conjugation. Real is *exact* there and halves memory. Note that it changes the model otherwise (O(n) instead of U(n), GOE instead of GUE) |
| complex | unitary dynamics e^{-iHt}, Lindblad with a Hamiltonian, Haar U(n) / CUE / CSE / SU(n), random pure states, broken time reversal |
| single | Monte Carlo statistics whose statistical error is far above 1e-6, exploratory optimisation |
| double | effects that are small compared with the operators: inequalities near equality, small gaps, entropies of nearly pure states, contrast near 1, exact-result checks, long RK4 runs. On consumer GPUs FP64 is 32-64x slower than FP32 |

`complex_valued=False` gives real algebras (orthogonal instead of unitary groups); complex input with a non-zero
imaginary part is rejected there. Each `force_*` flag (`self_adjoint`, `positive`, `normal`, `invertible`,
`projection`) tags the operator after checking it against the sampled eigenvalues. The `batch_size` argument of
`TypeIAlgebra` only sets the batch of its Hilbert-space bases; every factory takes its own `batch_size`.

More in [`examples/`](examples): random Hamiltonian with a parity symmetry, free additive
convolution, a trace inequality, unitary ensembles, a Zipf density matrix, decoherence inside
superselection sectors, the Michelson contrast under unital and non-unital channels, the
spectral form factor of GUE vs. Poisson Hamiltonians, inclusions and their indices (`example_inclusions.py`),
Schur–Weyl duality from the symmetries alone (`example_decompose.py`), SU(2) multiplets of the Heisenberg ring
(`example_su2.py`), Rényi divergences with data processing and recovery (`example_divergences.py`), and Jordan
algebras and spin factors (`example_jordan.py`).

- Scripts and the parts of the paper they produce: `validate_known_results` (Section 5), `numerics` (Section 3),
  `inequality_search` and `bounds_search` (Section 6), `renyi_threshold` (Section 6.1: the data-processing thresholds
  of the sandwiched and Petz Rényi divergences, located by batched optimisation with violations certified in mpmath;
  about 2 minutes in quick mode on one CPU thread), `connes_stormer` (Section 6, use case: the Connes–Størmer
  relative entropy of two maximal abelian subalgebras of M_n, closed form for n = 2 and the observation that
  orthonormal bases are not always optimal for n ≥ 3; about 12 minutes in full mode on a CPU), `chains` (Section 7), `baselines` (Section 9),
  `benchmark_library` (Section 10), `permutational` (use case: a boundary time crystal of N two-level systems under
  local emission and dephasing, N up to 80 on a CPU and 200 on a GPU), `trace_characterizations` (use case: how strongly inequalities that characterise
  the trace fail for Tr(W·) as a function of the Michelson contrast of W; about 5 minutes on a CPU), `symmetry_resolved`
  (use case: symmetry-resolved entanglement of XXZ ground states, ρ_A in ⊕_q M_{binom(ℓ,q)} without forming |ψ⟩⟨ψ|;
  L = 24 in about 20 minutes on a CPU).
- The driver first prints the GPU, its memory, CUDA and cuDNN versions (also saved to
  `man/article/generated/logs/environment.txt`) and stops if CUDA is not available.
- Every script writes its own log to `man/article/generated/logs/<script>.log`, including run-time estimates
  from the `cost` module. A failing script does not stop the others; the run ends with a summary.
- `ONLY="chains baselines" bash scripts/run_all.sh full cuda` reruns a subset.
- `scripts/gpu_check.py` (about 10 minutes, most of it the CPU side of the timings) has three parts:
  (1) correctness: the same inputs on the CPU and the GPU, all operations of the paper, single and double precision,
  Haar sampling (Gram-Schmidt on the GPU against QR, moments); (2) structure: host synchronisations and kernel
  launches per operation must not grow with the number of sectors (C = 1, 16, 256 at a fixed total size);
  (3) timings against all CPU cores, with the verdict on the hypothesis "the GPU is faster for large batches" and a
  comparison of the batched generation with the per-sector algorithm of the first full run. It exits with an
  error if (1) or (2) fails, and writes `gpu_check.json`. Run it first on a new machine or after changing the
  library. `tests/test_sync_free.py` checks the absence of host reads on the CPU, in the normal test suite.
- Duration. On an RTX 5090 (16-thread CPU) the first full run took about 5 hours: validate 12 min, numerics and
  baselines 3 min, benchmark 8 min, chains 7 min, inequality_search 3.4 h, bounds_search 1.3 h; most of
  inequality_search was Haar sampling with a per-matrix QR on the GPU, now batched. On a 4-thread CPU
  the `check` run takes about 33 minutes (validate 90 s, numerics 88 s, baselines 45 s, benchmark 189 s,
  chains 623 s, inequality_search 49 s, bounds_search 871 s; every log ends with a `[resources]` line).
- Interruptions. `inequality_search` and `bounds_search` save every finished part to `generated/partial/`; after a
  crash or reboot, rerunning the same command continues where it stopped and gives the same numbers.
- Several GPUs. The scripts are independent, e.g.
  `CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=0 ONLY=bounds_search bash scripts/run_all.sh full cuda` and
  `CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=1 ONLY="validate_known_results numerics baselines benchmark_library chains inequality_search" bash scripts/run_all.sh full cuda`
  in two terminals (the examples run only when `ONLY` is not set).
- `... full cuda tf32` repeats the run with TF32 tensor-core matrix products (Ampere or newer), to measure
  their effect; the default is true FP32.
- Results land in `man/article/generated/` (tables as `.tex` and `.json`) and `man/article/figures/`. Commit those
  two directories and the logs, then rebuild the PDF; the tables state the device and mode they came from.
- Memory. The largest single step is the dense diagonalisation of a 12870-dimensional sector in `chains`
  (about 10 GB on the CPU in the `check` run); use a GPU with at least 24 GB, preferably 40-80 GB, and a host with
  at least 16 GB RAM. The `check` run shows the peak GPU memory of every script in its `[resources]` line.
- Double precision is used throughout the validation and chain scripts; a data-centre GPU (A100, H100) is much
  faster there than a consumer GPU (FP64 at 1/2 versus 1/32-1/64 of the FP32 rate).

## Limitations

- SVD dominates the cost for `k_max ≳ 200` with large batches.
- `SpinChain(2, 'periodic')` has a single bond (the wrap bond is not counted twice), so it equals the open chain.
- `Channel`, Hamiltonians and Lindblad generators preserve the sectors; maps between sectors are
  `InterSectorChannel`s. Only abelian charges are fused. Of the non-abelian symmetries, SU(2) reduces the blocks of
  spin-1/2 chains (`SU2Chain`, dense up to L ≈ 16); SU(2) combined with momentum and chains of larger spins are not
  implemented. `decompose` handles any symmetry given by matrices, but densely (n up to a few hundred) and over C only.
  Operators on all sectors of a chain are stored padded (work in single sectors for large chains).
- Sandwiched Rényi divergences for small α < 1 of ill-conditioned states are not accurate (the sandwich
  σ^g ρ σ^g, g = (1-α)/(2α), cannot be resolved in floating point).
- `lindblad_channel`, `choi` and `superoperator` work with k_c² × k_c² matrices per block, which
  limits them to blocks of a few tens; use `lindblad_evolve` for larger blocks.
- Gradients of the functional calculus are finite at degenerate spectra; gradients through the eigenvectors
  returned by `eigh` are not. Second derivatives of f(A) are correct at non-degenerate spectra.
- `holomorphic_function` needs a circle that encloses the spectrum and on which f is holomorphic (the default circle
  suits entire functions such as exp); for self-adjoint or normal operators `apply_function` is exact and cheaper.
- On GPUs, batched Hermitian eigensolvers cover all block sizes only in recent PyTorch/cuSOLVER releases.
- Finite dimensions and Type I only.
