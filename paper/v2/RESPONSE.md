# Response to the reviewers

We thank both reviewers for their careful reading. The manuscript has been rewritten rather than
revised: the library itself was extended (states, channels within and between sectors, tensor products
and sector fusion, spin chains, Krylov solvers, dynamics, constrained optimisation, controllable number
field and precision, run-time/memory safeguards), and every number, table and figure of the new version
is regenerated by `scripts/paper/run_all.sh` (`bash scripts/paper/run_all.sh full cuda [tf32]`). Section and
table references below are to `paper/v2/main.tex`.

> **Note for the authors before resubmission.** The tables in the repository were produced on a CPU
> container in `quick` mode. Run `bash scripts/paper/run_all.sh full cuda` on a modern
> GPU (A100/H100/RTX 40xx), rebuild the PDF, and check that the GPU-dependent statements in

## Reviewer #1

**1. The introduction lacks background and does not state the objective.**
The Introduction (Section 1) is new. It defines the algebra in Eq. (1), lists where such algebras arise
(superselection rules and decoherence; symmetry sectors of many-body Hamiltonians; decoherence-free
subspaces, operator-algebra error correction, symmetry-preserving QAOA mixers; random-matrix ensembles;
equivariant layers in machine learning; inequalities between traces in operator theory), states what
existing software offers (Table 1), the concrete challenges (i)–(vii), and an explicit
"Objective and scope" paragraph followed by an outline.

**2. The meaning of n_c in the formula is not explained.**
Eq. (1) now defines C (number of direct summands), n_c (size of the c-th matrix factor) and k_c
(dimension of the subspace on which it acts, k_c ≤ n_c), and Section 2 repeats the notation with the
three traces.

**3. Why do NumPy/SciPy, Qiskit, QuTiP etc. not support this?**
They can, through the embedding M ⊂ M_N, N = Σ k_c, and the revised Introduction says so (paragraph
"Why not one large matrix?"). It then lists what the embedding loses and the user has to supply:
the cost (Σ k_c³ instead of N³, a factor C² for C equal blocks); the measures (the unitary group of M
is Π U(k_c), a null set in U(N), so Haar sampling and gradient steps in M_N leave M); the C-parameter
family of traces, on which densities and duals of maps between sectors depend; and the centre, which
carries the classical (superselected) part of the system. A new paragraph "From a symmetry to the
algebra" explains why physical models with a symmetry live in an algebra of this form (the commutant
of the symmetry). Table 1 compares features, and Section 8 quantifies the difference with NumPy/SciPy
(see Reviewer #2, point 6).

**4. Other applications, e.g. deep learning.**
Introduction, bullet "machine learning": by Schur's lemma, equivariant linear maps form exactly an
algebra of the form (1), with n_c the multiplicities of the irreducible representations (Cohen–Welling,
Finzi et al.); random-matrix analysis of trained weights (Martin–Mahoney) is also cited. Gradients flow
through all library operations (Section 3, challenge (iv); Section 6).

**5. A concrete quantum computing example.**
Section 5 ("Usage by example"), paragraph "A noisy quantum circuit with a conserved quantity", and `examples/paper/ex9_qaoa_xy_mixer.py`: an XY (Hamming-weight-preserving) mixer on
6 qubits as an operator in the sector algebra, T1 decay as a Lindblad jump *between* sectors and T2
dephasing within sectors, and exact post-selection on the correct Hamming weight via central
projections. The example is part of the test suite.

**6. Insufficient description of methods and implementation.**
Section 3 "Design and implementation" now describes the software stack, the four-step workflow, the
padded batched data layout and its cost, the object model (Table 3), and Table 2 maps each challenge to
the implementation (Haar sampling via QR with phase fix, SU(n)/COE/CSE constructions, spectral
parametrisations for gradients at degenerate spectra, exact contrast-constrained parametrisation,
ETA/memory estimates). A subsection on number field and precision gives timing and accuracy tables.

**7. The P100 is old; tensor cores are not discussed.**
Section 3, paragraph on GPUs: we discuss TF32 tensor cores, the `--tf32` switch of the scripts, when
TF32 is acceptable (sampling statistics) and when not (eigenvalues near degeneracies, validations), and
the scripts record GPU model, compute capability, CUDA/cuDNN version and matmul precision into every
generated table. Results are to be regenerated on a current GPU (see the note above).

**8. Underlying packages / design.**
Section 3, "Software stack": pure Python on PyTorch (torch.linalg → LAPACK/MKL on CPU,
cuSOLVER/cuBLAS/MAGMA on GPU, CSR sparse matrices, reverse-mode autodiff), SciPy, pandas, Matplotlib;
CI on Python 3.10 and 3.12.

**9. Figure captions, analysis and comparative discussion.**
All captions were rewritten to be self-contained (what is plotted, axes, sample sizes, what to read off).
Each figure is now discussed in the text, and Section 8 adds a direct comparison with NumPy/SciPy.

**10. GPU speedups are not encouraging.**
Section 10 discusses this openly: GPUs help when there is enough independent work per kernel (many
samples, many sectors, large blocks, matrix products), not for decompositions of a few small matrices.
The library is batched throughout for this reason, and the cost module warns before slow configurations.
For single small problems we recommend the CPU or SciPy, and say so.

**11. Related references.**
The bibliography was extended to 40 entries (operator algebras, superselection, decoherence, symmetry
in exact diagonalisation, QEC, QAOA, random matrices, equivariant networks, and the software packages of
Table 1); every entry is cited in the text.

## Reviewer #2

**1. The conclusions drawn from the Monte Carlo study contradict the figures.**
Monte Carlo sampling was replaced by constrained optimisation (Section 6): with the Michelson contrast
fixed exactly by the parametrisation, gradient ascent/descent finds the extremal values, which bound
the sampled clouds from both sides. Section 6 shows that sampling misses the bounds in all but the
smallest dimensions, and states only conclusions that the optimisation supports.

**2. Benchmark inconsistencies (batch sizes, single-threaded CPU, figure vs. table).**
The figure and the table of Section 10 are produced by the same script run from the same measurements;
the batch size is chosen per configuration to hold about 2^22 entries and is printed in the table; the
CPU runs with all available threads and the thread count is recorded in the caption; timings exclude
warm-up and synchronise the GPU.

**3. Validation is limited.**
Section 7 validates against exact results: Haar moments and Weingarten integrals, Page's formula,
purities of random states, spontaneous emission, free-fermion spectra and dynamics, Heisenberg ring
energies L = 4…16, SU(2) multiplet nesting, domain-wall melting, Peschel's entanglement formula,
GOE/Poisson level-spacing ratios, momentum-sector spectra, the Marshall sign rule, binomial loss
statistics and exact post-selection. All are part of the test suite (262 tests).

**4. The three traces are not validated.**
New Table "trace functionals" (Section 7): for Tr_blunt, Tr_norm and τ_vN on random operators in
M_2 ⊕ M_3 ⊕ M_5 (double precision, 2000 samples) we check normalisation on the identity, the trace
property f(AB) = f(BA), unitary invariance f(UAU*) = f(A), positivity f(A*A) > 0, and the identity
Tr_norm = C·τ_vN. Density conversions between traces and the duals of channels with respect to each
trace are covered by the test suite.

**5. Precision is not reported.**
Subsection "Number field and precision" (Section 3): the library runs in real or complex arithmetic,
single or double precision (`TypeIAlgebra(..., complex_valued=..., precision=...)`); two tables give
time and accuracy for each choice, and the text states when real/single suffices and when complex or
double is required. Every generated table records the precision used.

**6. Novelty compared with plain PyTorch/NumPy.**
Section 8 "Comparison with NumPy/SciPy" implements the same computations three ways: a straightforward
NumPy/SciPy loop, hand-vectorised NumPy, and the library. The library matches hand-vectorised NumPy on
the CPU and is 60–600× faster than loops; for single ground states ARPACK (`eigsh`) is faster than our
batched Lanczos on the CPU, which we report. The contribution is not new kernels but the correct and
validated bookkeeping (sectors, traces, duals, Haar measures, constraints) in one batched, differentiable
interface; Table 2 lists what a user would otherwise reimplement.

**7. Mathematical inaccuracies.**
Section 2 was rewritten: definitions of the three traces with weights, states as densities with respect
to Tr_blunt and the conversion rules, completely positive maps between sectors with their duals per
trace, tensor products and fusion, Lindblad dynamics. Errors of the first version (non-Haar unitary
sampler, mislabelled ensembles) are corrected and listed in `paper/CHANGES.md`.

**8. The abstract is a bullet list.**
The abstract is now continuous prose.

**9. URL inconsistency.**
The repository URL is `https://github.com/andre-a-no/von_neumann_type_i` everywhere (program summary
and availability section).

**10. Captions.**
See Reviewer #1, point 9.

**11. References not cited.**
Every bibliography entry is now cited in the text (checked automatically).
