\subsection{Tomography in the algebra as training}\label{sec:learn-tomo}

Process tomography by maximum likelihood is training in the sense of this section: the model is a parametrised
channel, the data are measurement counts on the outputs of known inputs, and the loss is the negative
log-likelihood. Learned models of this kind are used for process tomography with tensor
networks~\cite{torlai2023} and for state tomography with neural-network states~\cite{torlai2018}. The algebra enters
twice. First, it fixes what has to be measured. If $\M\subset B(\mathcal H)$ is a unital $*$-subalgebra, then
$\Tr(\rho A)=\Tr(E_\M(\rho)A)$ for $A\in\M$, where $E_\M$ is the trace-preserving conditional expectation, and the
pairing $(\sigma,A)\mapsto\Tr(\sigma A)$ is nondegenerate on $\M$ (take $A=\sigma^*$). Measurements of observables of
$\M$ therefore determine exactly $E_\M(\rho)$, and a process that maps $\M$ into $\M$, such as the noisy register of
Section~\ref{sec:noisy}, is determined on $\M$ by inputs in $\M$ and measurements in $\M$. Permutationally invariant
tomography~\cite{toth2010} is the case of the commutant of the permutations. Second, the model can respect the
sectors. A channel of $\M=\bigoplus_NM_{\binom nN}$ ranges over a set of dimension $(\dim\M)^2-\dim\M$, which is
$\CLSetAlg$ for $n=\CLnMax$, against $4^{2n}-4^n=\CLSetDense$ for a channel of $M_{2^n}$. A GKSL generator whose jumps
change $N$ by at most one has fewer parameters still. We measure what each level of structure buys, with the same
simulated experiment for all models.

\paragraph{Setup.} The register is the ring of Section~\ref{sec:noisy} with $n=\CLnMin$ and $n=\CLnMax$ qubits. The
XY mixer acts for a time $T=\CLT$, together with amplitude damping, thermal excitation and dephasing at the rates
$\gamma_\downarrow=\CLGdown$, $\gamma_\uparrow=\CLGup$ and $\gamma_\varphi=\CLGphi$. The true process
$\Phi=e^{T\mathcal L}$ is computed by \texttt{lindblad\_evolve} with the exact exponential. A dense Lindblad evolution in
$M_{2^n}$, with $H$ and the jumps converted by \texttt{interop}, agrees with it to $\CLDenseCheck$. The training
inputs are the $2^n$ basis states and $\binom nN^2$ Haar-random pure states in every sector with $\binom nN>1$. They
span $\M$, and
there are $\CLProbes$ of them for $n=\CLnMax$. The held-out inputs are $\CLTest$ further states: three pure states in each
of these sectors and eight random mixed states with weight on all sectors. Each output is measured in $\CLSettingsAlg$
Haar-random bases of $\M$ (\texttt{POVM.from\_unitary} of block-diagonal unitaries, combined with equal weights by
\texttt{POVM.combine}). Every outcome of such a basis also reveals $N$, so the charge measurement
\texttt{POVM.sectors} is a coarse-graining of each setting. The counts are sampled with $10^2$ to $10^5$ shots per
input in total, and the exact probabilities serve as the limit of infinitely many shots. Four models are fitted:
\begin{itemize}
\item[(i)] \emph{rates}: the noise model with known jump operators and four unknowns $J,\gamma_\downarrow,
\gamma_\uparrow,\gamma_\varphi$ (the coupling $J$ multiplies the mixer);
\item[(ii)] \emph{GKSL layer}: a \texttt{LindbladLayer} with a free self-adjoint $H\in\M$, $n-1$ free jumps inside the
sectors and $n$ free jumps between neighbouring sectors $N\to N\pm1$; it contains the true generator;
\item[(iii)] \emph{channel of $\M$}: a \texttt{ChannelLayer} from $\M$ to $\M$ with all sector transitions and Kraus
rank $k_{\max}^2$, enough for every channel of $\M$;
\item[(iv)] \emph{channel of $M_{2^n}$}: a \texttt{ChannelLayer} on $M_{2^n}$ with Kraus rank $4^n$, enough for every
channel. This is the experimenter who ignores the symmetry: the same inputs, embedded by \texttt{interop.to\_dense},
are measured in $\CLSettingsDense$ Haar-random bases of $M_{2^n}$, with the same number of shots per input. The model
has to learn that the process creates no coherences between sectors.
\end{itemize}
The raw parameter counts (\texttt{learn.count\_parameters}, Table~\ref{tab:learn-tomo}) include the Kraus gauge; the
sets of channels that models (iii) and (iv) can reach have dimensions $\CLSetAlg$ and $\CLSetDense$, and the inputs
in $\M$ identify $\CLIdentDense$ of the latter. Each model is fitted by $\CLAdam$ Adam steps followed by at most
$\CLLbfgs$ iterations of L-BFGS (both from \texttt{torch.optim}), with $\CLStartsRates$, $\CLStartsLindblad$,
$\CLStartsAlgebra$ and $\CLStartsDense$ random starts for models (i)--(iv), and the start with the smallest loss is
kept. The losses of the starts agree to a relative $\CLStartSpread$ in every run with finite shots. During training the
outputs are computed by equivalent fast paths: the exponential of the generator as a matrix on $\M$, of size
$\dim\M=\CLDimAlg$, and Kraus operators applied to the pure input vectors. These agree with the forward passes of the
layers to $\CLFastPath$. Evaluation uses the layers themselves. The whole study takes $\CLRuntime$ minutes on
$\CLWorkers$ CPU cores.

\paragraph{Results.} The models differ by large factors only where the structure is strong
(Figure~\ref{fig:learn-tomo}a). At $10^4$ shots per input and $n=\CLnMax$, the held-out trace distance is
$\CLTDRatesFour$ for the rate model, $\CLTDLindbladFour$ for the GKSL layer, $\CLTDAlgebraFour$ for the channel of $\M$
and $\CLTDDenseFour$ for the dense channel. The rate model follows the law $\text{shots}^{-1/2}$ (fitted slope
$\CLSlopeRates$ over the four budgets). The larger models fall more slowly in this range, with slopes $\CLSlopeLindblad$,
$\CLSlopeAlgebra$ and $\CLSlopeDense$, so they are not yet in their asymptotic regime and the gaps between them grow
with the number of shots. The structure of the algebra is worth more than an order of magnitude in shots: the dense
model needs $\CLNeedDense$ shots per input to reach the error $\CLTarget$ that the channel of $\M$ reaches with
$\CLNeedAlgebra$, a factor $\CLNeedRatio$. A large part of its error has a simple origin. Its predictions contain
coherences between sectors of trace norm $\CLOffDense$ (at $10^4$ shots), which the true process never creates and
which the channel of $\M$ excludes by construction. The GKSL layer gains much less over the channel of $\M$ than its
parameter count suggests: it needs $\CLNeedLindblad$ shots for the same error although it has $\CLParRatio$ times
fewer raw parameters. For so small a register, Markovianity and nearest-neighbour jumps constrain little. Knowing the
jump operators is what pays: the rate model is below the target error already at $10^2$ shots ($\CLTDRatesTwo$). The
fits to the exact probabilities reach $\CLTDLindbladExact$, $\CLTDAlgebraExact$ and $\CLTDDenseExact$ for models
(ii)--(iv), far below the statistical errors, so the comparison at finite shots is not limited by the optimiser. The
same ordering holds for the Choi error and for $n=\CLnMin$, where the GKSL layer and the channel of $\M$ tie at
$10^4$ shots.

\paragraph{Learned rates.} For the rate model the learned values at $\CLRepeatShots$ shots are $J=\CLRatesJ\pm
\CLRatesJSd$, $\gamma_\downarrow=\CLRatesDown\pm\CLRatesDownSd$, $\gamma_\uparrow=\CLRatesUp\pm\CLRatesUpSd$ and
$\gamma_\varphi=\CLRatesPhi\pm\CLRatesPhiSd$ (mean and standard deviation over $\CLRepeats$ data sets). The
Cram\'er--Rao standard deviations, from the Fisher information of the counts at the true parameters
(\texttt{measurement.fisher\_information}), are $\CLCRJ$, $\CLCRDown$, $\CLCRUp$
and $\CLCRPhi$, so the maximum-likelihood estimator is unbiased and close to efficient here (with six data sets the
empirical spread is itself uncertain by about a third). The GKSL layer is not a rate estimator. Its generator,
projected onto the physical terms, gives $\gamma_\downarrow=\CLLindbladDown\pm\CLLindbladDownSd$,
$\gamma_\uparrow=\CLLindbladUp\pm\CLLindbladUpSd$ and $\gamma_\varphi=\CLLindbladPhi\pm\CLLindbladPhiSd$. These are
biased by more than their spread, and a fraction $\CLResidual$ of the learned generator lies outside the physical span:
the free jumps absorb statistical noise as extra dissipation, which the projection attributes in part to the rates.
This fraction falls with the number of shots (from about $0.19$ at $10^2$ to $0.01$ at $10^5$ shots per input, one data set
each), and from the exact probabilities the GKSL layer recovers the rates to a relative $10^{-4}$ or better.

\paragraph{Scope.} The data are simulated without state-preparation and measurement errors, the inputs and
measurement bases are known exactly, and the true process lies inside every model class: it is Markovian and
respects the sectors exactly. The experiment therefore measures statistical efficiency, not robustness to a wrong
model. With noise that breaks the $U(1)$ symmetry, models (i)--(iii) would be biased and the dense model would not,
which is where the dense model would win; we did not simulate this case. The dense comparison changes the measurement
together with the model, as an experimenter without the symmetry would; we did not separate the two effects. The
registers are small ($n\le\CLnMax$, one evolution time), and every fit is a local optimisation. For the GKSL layer the
start matters: with the library's default initial Hamiltonian of unit scale, only $\CLInitConverged$ of
$\CLInitStarts$ starts reached the optimum for the exact data at $n=\CLnMax$, and the others stopped at losses up to
$\CLInitLoss$. We therefore start it near the identity channel, with the Hamiltonian scaled by $\CLHScale$, and with
this choice all starts agree. The full-rank models cost one parameter per entry of a Stinespring isometry. At
$n=5$ the dense model would have $4^{10}\approx10^6$ complex Kraus entries, which is why the study stops at
$n=\CLnMax$; the GKSL layer and the channel of $\M$ would still be practical there.
